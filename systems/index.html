Isolating the house bank from the inverter bank is a sound design, but the isolated side still needs a way in — a DC-DC charger off the main chassis, its own controller, or an alternator feed. The specification names 25.6 V charging equipment only, so the dashed link above is what the design requires, not what is known to be fitted.

The array now outruns the show. A two-hour broadcast — encoder, interface, Starlink, cameras, lighting and monitoring — draws about 366 W, or 0.81 kWh at the bank. Even a poor solar day covers that a dozen times over, which is what 4,000 W bought: the generator stops being part of the broadcast plan and goes back to being a backup.

The broadcast chain

Seven angles and a stereo pair go in. One directed stream comes out.

Before the Producer, switching was manual: one operator, one angle at a time, and the wide shot whenever nobody was free to cut. The Producer sits between the sources and the encoder and makes that decision continuously — which is what turns six fixed cameras and a phone into something that reads as a multi-camera broadcast rather than a webcam pointed at a boat.

The Producer

A directing layer above Deckner, not a replacement for it.

Deckner is the vessel's agent: it listens to the cameras' substreams, the NMEA bus and the sensors, and it knows what is happening aboard. It has never been a director — it has no opinion about which shot is interesting. The Producer is that opinion. It takes Deckner's picture of the boat, adds its own read of the frame, and drives the studio.

The split matters for the same reason the rest of the boat is split: a bad call in the directing layer should cost a dull cut, not a missed engine alarm. Deckner keeps running if the Producer stops. The Producer has no path to the helm — it can only reach the studio.

Framing

Per source, continuously
Track
Follows the performer across the frame and drives the pan-tilt head to keep them composed rather than centred.
Zoom
Pushes in for a solo or a held note and pulls back out before the shot gets claustrophobic.

Cutting

Across all seven angles
Select
Chooses which camera is live — including the handheld, which is treated as an angle rather than a special case.
Pace
Holds a shot long enough to read and cuts before it goes stale. Static framing for minutes at a time is the thing that made the old stream feel like a security feed.

Look

Applied before the encoder
Grade
Colour-corrects each source so six cameras from one budget batch and a phone do not visibly disagree about what white is — the single biggest tell in a multi-camera cut.
Overlay
Draws lower thirds and live ocean telemetry, composited with the Lyric Show captions before encoding so nothing desyncs downstream.

Boundaries. The Producer commands the studio over a local socket and nothing else. It cannot reach the autopilot, the helm display or the navigation bus — that path does not exist in its code, which is a structural guarantee rather than a configuration setting. Deckner holds the same restriction independently.

Handheld · Continuity Camera

Wherever the show is
What it adds
A moving, close, human angle — walking the deck, out on the swim platform, into the crowd on the dock. The fixed rig cannot do any of that.
How it connects
Wirelessly, as a native camera device on the studio host. No capture card, no cable through the transom door.
How it is treated
As one more selectable angle. The Producer cuts to it on the same logic it uses for the fixed six.

This is the piece that changes the format. Six locked-off cameras produce coverage; a camera that can walk produces a show.

Machines get the cheapest adequate feed; full quality is reserved for the output people actually watch. Tracking keeps working at frame rate while the encoder is saturating the uplink.

Audio

Stereo aboard; the performance close-miked on top of it.

Stereo pair

Aboard, fixed

A left/right pair capturing the room: the water, the dock, the audience, the boat itself. It gives the stream a place instead of a dry voice on a black background, and it is what lets a cut to the handheld still sound like the same show.

Close capture

Interface at the stage

Vocal microphone and instrument DI into the USB-C interface — console mic pre and JFET DI at 24-bit / 96 kHz, bus-powered from the studio host. This is the performance; the stereo pair is the space around it.

One device, two consumers

Studio host

The interface presents as a single system audio input, so the encoder and Lyric Show read the same source without a second cable or a loopback. Captions are generated from the same audio the audience hears.

Helm, navigation and instruments

One NMEA 2000 backbone. Everything downstream listens; nothing writes.
  • Multifunction display with broadband radar — primary helm screen, radar overlaid for collision avoidance.
  • In-hull depth transducer — reads through solid fibreglass from inside the bilge. Nothing protrudes, which matters on Keys sandbars.
  • Navigation bridge — GPS, heading, pitch and roll, and depth, published to both the helm display and the studio network.
  • Autopilot — commanded over NMEA by the helm display and navigation software, and by nothing else.
  • Tide and weather station — NOAA tide curves, buoy data and wind vectors, which is also where the ocean-telemetry overlay gets its numbers.

Read-only, structurally. Neither Deckner nor the Producer can write to the helm. That boundary is architectural, not a setting — nothing in either code path can reach the autopilot or the course plotter.

Network

One uplink, one firewall, one flat segment — and the reasons that is temporary.
Architecture only — addresses, identifiers and live configuration are deliberately omitted.

The uplink feeds a firewall, the firewall feeds one switch, and everything hangs off that switch. The firewall's WAN sits behind the uplink's own router, so the boat runs double NAT. That is fine while nothing needs to be reachable from outside; if inbound ports are ever required, the uplink goes into bypass rather than forwarding at two layers.

Both switches are unmanaged, so the network cannot be split at the switch. Adding a segment means adding a port at the router — a USB-to-Ethernet adapter becomes a third interface, and camera isolation goes there. Address planning matters more than usual afloat: the LAN was moved off the uplink router's default range entirely, because the same subnet on both sides of a firewall breaks routing in ways that look like random connectivity loss.

Known single point of failure. One box carries routing, Wi-Fi, DNS, camera access and the stream. If it dies, all of them go at once. Worth knowing before it matters.

Open work

Ranked by blast radius. Specifics live in the private build sheet, not here.

01 · Two resolvers on one segment

Root cause

A router that was meant to be demoted to an access point is still routing, and still answering for both address assignment and name resolution. Clients that lease from the wrong one get the wrong gateway, which is why devices intermittently stop seeing each other.

Done looks like: the old box is an access point and nothing else — no address assignment, no name resolution, one uplink into the switch, its own WAN port unused.

02 · Firewall admin unreachable

Locks out the fix

The firewall's own admin interface does not answer from the LAN. It times out rather than refusing, which points at a rule or a binding rather than a dead service — but it also means the first fault cannot be corrected from a browser.

Done looks like: console access at the box, the anti-lockout rule restored, and the firewall the only thing on the network assigning addresses or answering names.

03 · The studio host exposes nothing

Blocks the Producer

Remote administration, the studio control socket and the local model endpoint are each either closed or bound to loopback. Until that changes, the agent host cannot reach any of them, and the directing layer is a design rather than a running system.

Done looks like: remote login enabled, the studio control socket listening, and the local model endpoint reachable from the agent host.
