Vessel systems

How the boat is put together: power, navigation, surveillance, network and broadcast — and the reasoning behind each choice. Addresses, hardware identifiers and current fault state are deliberately omitted; this page documents architecture, not configuration. The same systems are drawn as animated schematics on the systems console.

Topology

A single uplink feeds a firewall, which feeds one switch. Everything else hangs off that switch on one flat segment. No VLANs yet — the constraint is that both switches are unmanaged, so the network cannot be split at the switch.

Starlink Roam
 └─ OPNsense firewall          gateway · DHCP · DNS
     └─ 8-port gigabit switch  LAN spine
         ├─ workstation        the Producer + broadcast + media
         ├─ access point       boat-wide Wi-Fi · handheld camera
         ├─ navigation bridge  N2K telemetry
         ├─ camera NVR         PoE, 6 cameras
         └─ agent host         sensors + automation

The firewall's WAN sits behind Starlink's own router, so the boat runs double NAT. That is fine while nothing needs to be reachable from outside; if inbound ports are ever required, Starlink goes into Bypass mode rather than adding forwards at two layers.

Known single point of failure. One box carries routing, Wi-Fi, DNS, camera access and the stream. If it dies, all of them go at once. Worth knowing before it matters.

Power

Generation

A 4,000 W flexible solar array carries the standing load and the show on top of it — eight panels, four in series and two strings in parallel, into a single 140 A MPPT controller. An 8,000 W inverter generator remains aboard for heavy AC draw and fast recharge, but at this array size it is a backup rather than part of the broadcast plan.

Storage

Two banks, kept apart. A 25.6 V bank of two 600 Ah LiFePO₄ units in series — 15.4 kWh nameplate, about 13.8 kWh usable — sits behind the 4,000 W inverter and carries the 120 V side. A separate 12.8 V 320 Ah bank — 4.1 kWh nameplate — is reserved for DC house loads and charged from the main chassis through an isolating DC-DC converter.

Separating them is the point. A long broadcast is allowed to draw the boat's stored energy down; it is not allowed to flatten the bank that runs the navigation lights, the bilge pumps and the network. The agent host was still chosen for single-digit idle watts rather than raw speed, because the DC side has to survive the nights when nothing is generating.

What a show costs

Encoder, interface, uplink, cameras, lighting and monitoring come to roughly 366 W of AC draw — about 0.81 kWh at the bank across a two-hour set. Even a poor solar day harvests a dozen times that. That is the practical change 4,000 W bought: the generator stopped being something the broadcast schedule had to plan around.

The isolated DC bank still has no confirmed charging path. The specified charging equipment is all 25.6 V, so the house side needs a DC-DC charger from the main chassis, its own controller, or an alternator feed. Deliberate isolation is a sound design; isolation without a way in is a flat bank.

Helm and navigation

Instruments share an NMEA 2000 backbone.

  • Multifunction display with broadband radar — primary helm screen, radar overlaid for collision avoidance.
  • In-hull depth transducer — shoots through solid fibreglass from inside the bilge. Nothing protrudes, which matters on Keys sandbars.
  • Navigation bridge — GPS, heading, pitch and roll, and depth, published to both the helm display and the LAN.
  • Autopilot — commanded over NMEA by the helm display and navigation software.
  • Tide and weather station — NOAA tide curves, buoy data and wind vectors over Wi-Fi.

The agent is read-only on navigation. It listens to the bus and records decoded messages, and has no command authority over the autopilot or course plotting. That boundary is architectural, not a setting — nothing in the agent's code path can write to the helm.

Vision and security

Six 5 MP pan-tilt cameras run off the recorder's built-in PoE, which keeps them on the recorder's own segment instead of the main network. Six budget cameras running vendor firmware do not belong on the same segment as the workstation and the firewall's admin interface.

Coverage splits between inward views for performance capture and outward views for waterline and dock approaches, with elevated positions for the wide master shot.

The handheld

A phone running Continuity Camera joins the rig wirelessly as a native camera device on the studio host — no capture card, and no cable through the transom door. It walks the deck, the swim platform and the dock, and the Producer treats it as one more selectable angle rather than a special case. Six locked-off cameras produce coverage; a camera that can move produces a show.

Stream separation

The agent pulls substreams over RTSP for motion detection and attention routing. The broadcast encoder takes mainstreams. Splitting them means situational awareness and the live stream never compete for the same bandwidth — the agent keeps working at full frame rate while the encoder is saturating the uplink.

Audio

A stereo pair aboard captures the room — the water, the dock, the audience and the boat itself. It gives the stream a place instead of a dry voice on a black background, and it is what lets a cut to the handheld still sound like the same show rather than a different recording.

The performance sits on top of that: vocal microphone and instrument DI into a USB-C interface at 24-bit / 96 kHz, bus-powered from the studio host. The interface presents as a single system audio input, so the encoder and the caption overlay read the same source without a second cable or a loopback — captions are generated from the audio the audience actually hears.

Core networking

The firewall handles routing, DNS over TLS and network-level ad-blocking. Because both switches are unmanaged, adding a segment means adding a port at the router: a USB-to-Ethernet adapter with a Realtek RTL8153 or ASIX AX88179 chipset works without extra drivers and becomes a third interface. Camera isolation goes there.

Address planning matters more than usual afloat. Starlink's router hands out a common private range, so the LAN was moved off that range entirely — same subnet on both sides of a firewall breaks routing in ways that look like random connectivity loss.

Direction

Two layers, deliberately separate.

The vessel agent

The agent ingests camera substreams, multi-track audio and navigation telemetry to drive vision tracking and situational awareness. It knows what is happening aboard. It has never had an opinion about which shot is interesting, and it is read-only on navigation.

The Producer

The Producer is that opinion. It sits above the agent and drives the studio over a local WebSocket: it tracks a performer across the frame and commands the pan-tilt head to keep them composed rather than merely centred, pushes in on a moment and pulls back out before the shot gets claustrophobic, chooses which of the seven angles is live, paces the cuts so none of them goes stale, colour-corrects each source so a batch of budget cameras and a phone do not visibly disagree about what white is, and draws the lower thirds and live ocean-telemetry overlays. Output encodes to YouTube and Twitch over the satellite uplink.

The split follows the same fault-isolation rule as the rest of the boat: a bad call in the directing layer should cost a dull cut, not a missed engine alarm. The agent keeps running if the Producer stops, and neither has a code path to the helm — the Producer can reach the studio and nothing else.

Offline, the same machine hosts a large emulation library — the boat is frequently out of coverage, and entertainment that depends on the uplink is entertainment that stops working at anchor.

Water sports

Two surface-supplied hookah dive systems recharge off the house bank, giving roughly two hours of bottom time without tanks. Knee, wake and surf boards cover open-water runs; clear-bottom kayaks and paddleboards are for reef viewing at anchor.

Operating inside the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. A 20″ × 24″ dive flag is required whenever the rigs are in the water, and there is no spearfishing and no take inside sanctuary zones.

Design principles

Fault isolation over a single loop

Each capability runs as its own service rather than as one scheduler. A slow API call in the reasoning layer must never delay an engine alarm, and the only reliable way to guarantee that is to keep them in separate processes with separate failure modes.

Offline-first

Full-text search over local storage rather than a hosted vector index. At sea the uplink is intermittent by nature, so any component that assumes connectivity is a component that fails exactly when it is needed.

Identity in version control

The agent's personality, configuration and knowledge live in git, not on a disk. That makes the whole thing hardware-portable and survivable — a dead SD card costs an afternoon, not the project.

Substreams for machines, mainstreams for humans

Stated above, but it generalises: give automated consumers the cheapest adequate feed and reserve full quality for the output anyone actually watches.

Method

Network state here is measured rather than assumed. A build sheet records what was intended; live capture records what is running, and on this boat the two have disagreed more than once. The general technique, without the specifics:

  • Read the actual DHCP lease on an interface rather than trusting the configured address — that is how you catch a second DHCP server.
  • Enumerate hosts by multicast DNS service browsing, which names them instead of leaving you with a list of numbers.
  • Distinguish closed from filtered: a closed port refuses instantly, a firewalled one times out. Conflating the two sends you debugging a service that was never the problem.
  • Query each host directly for a public name to find resolvers that should not be answering.

Every one of those is a two-second check that has, at least once, saved an afternoon.
